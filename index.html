02 — SEASONAL DISCOVERY

New seasonal records reveal two different patterns

Citizen-science observations are not only adding more butterfly records. They are also documenting some species during months outside their previously recorded seasonal windows.

Across all species, these new records concentrate in particular months of the year. Looking at individual butterflies, however, reveals that the pattern differs considerably between species.

How widespread are the seasonal additions?

Each dot represents one butterfly species in the seasonal-record file, grouped by its number of newly documented months. Dots wrap into rows of five; labels show the total species in each group. Hover over a dot to see the species and months.

These records extend documented seasonal knowledge. They do not, on their own, demonstrate population growth or a climate-driven change in butterfly activity.

03 — CONSERVATION RISK

What does Australia's threatened-insect list contain?

Seasonal records help reveal when insects are active. Conservation listings answer a different question: which insects are formally recognised as threatened nationally?

This treemap groups the insects in the government extract by order, a scientific grouping of related insects. Within each group, colour separates the national threat categories. Larger coloured rectangles contain more listed taxa.

Conservation risk across insect groups

Each labelled group is an insect order. Lepidoptera includes butterflies and moths;

Coleoptera means beetles; Hymenoptera includes bees, wasps and ants.

Hover over a coloured rectangle for its full threat category and count. Rectangle labels show counts, with status codes where space allows.

CR = Critically Endangered; EN = Endangered; VU = Vulnerable.

A taxon is a listed species or subspecies. Counts describe the national EPBC Act list in this extract, not insect abundance or the proportion of all insects that are threatened. An unrepresented group does not necessarily have no species at risk.

How do insect group, threat and geography connect?

Read from left to right: insect order, national threat category, then the number of states or territories listed for each taxon.

Wider ribbons represent more taxa, and ribbon colour follows the threat category. Hover over a ribbon to inspect its complete pathway.

Click a vertical block to highlight its pathways; click it again or double-click the chart to clear the highlight.

CR = Critically Endangered; EN = Endangered; VU = Vulnerable. A taxon is a listed species or subspecies. One state or territory means one association in this extract; it does not measure range area or prove that a taxon occurs nowhere else. Ribbons connect classifications of the same taxa, rather than showing movement between places or changes over time.

Most listed insects have a single jurisdiction association

The previous chart separates taxa by the number of listed jurisdictions. This chart reveals the exact combinations: which states or territories are listed together?

Read each column vertically. Its bar counts the taxa associated with exactly the combination shown below. Teal dots identify included jurisdictions; pale dots identify jurisdictions outside that combination. All teal dots in one column belong to the same combination.

Hover over a bar or dot to highlight its column and see the combination, count and scientific names.

Each taxon contributes to one column only. For example, a column with teal dots only at NSW and QLD contains taxa associated with both jurisdictions and no others in this extract. Jurisdiction associations describe the source listing; they do not measure abundance, range area or movement, or establish that a taxon occurs nowhere else.

NSW = New South Wales; VIC = Victoria; QLD = Queensland; SA = South Australia; WA = Western Australia; TAS = Tasmania; NT = Northern Territory; ACT = Australian Capital Territory; OT = Other Territories. Rows containing only pale dots have no associations among the listed taxa in this extract.

04 — THE GEOGRAPHY OF CONSERVATION

Where is the share of Critically Endangered listed insects highest?

The same national list looks different across Australia. This map compares the share of listed insect taxa associated with each state or territory that are Critically Endangered, the most severe threat category in this extract.

Darker regions have a higher percentage. Read the counts alongside the percentage: a large share can come from a small number of listed taxa.

Critically Endangered share of each jurisdiction's listed insects

Percentage = Critically Endangered taxa ÷ all nationally listed insect taxa associated with that jurisdiction. Labels show the percentage and, where space allows, the count used to calculate it. Hover over a region or label to see its threat-category breakdown.

CR = Critically Endangered; EN = Endangered; VU = Vulnerable. A taxon is a listed species or subspecies. Grey means no listed insect taxa in this extract, so a percentage cannot be calculated. A region showing 0% has listed taxa, with none classified as Critically Endangered.

This is a comparison of the national listing extract, not the probability that an insect is threatened, a measure of population size, or a ranking of overall ecological risk. A taxon can be associated with more than one jurisdiction. The map uses an equal-area Albers projection and shows mainland Australia and Tasmania; offshore islands and Other Territories are omitted at this scale. ACT has a locator dot and an external label because it is small.

Where are more nationally listed insect taxa associated?

The previous map compares percentages. This map adds the counts behind them. Circle area represents the number of distinct nationally listed insect taxa associated with each state or territory; the number inside each circle gives the exact count.

Hover over a circle, label or region to see the jurisdiction's threat-category breakdown. A cross means no listed insect taxa in this extract. Circles for ACT, Victoria and Tasmania are moved slightly for readability; leader lines connect them to their jurisdictions.

Circle area, rather than radius, is proportional to the count. A taxon is a listed species or subspecies. Each taxon is counted once within each associated jurisdiction, so taxa listed in multiple jurisdictions appear in multiple counts. Adding the circles does not give the national total.

The circles summarise jurisdiction associations; their positions are not insect observation locations. Counts do not measure population size, range area, survey effort or the proportion of all insects that are threatened. No listed taxa does not mean no insects or no conservation concerns. The equal-area Albers map shows mainland Australia and Tasmania; offshore islands and Other Territories are omitted at this scale. Listings involving offshore islands remain included in their parent jurisdiction's count.

Listing data: DCCEEW — EPBC Act Threatened Species State Lists. Counts, percentages and the extract date are calculated from the supplied CSV files.

Boundaries and jurisdiction codes: Australian Bureau of Statistics — ASGS Edition 4 (2026). Generalised boundaries are simplified for this national view.
